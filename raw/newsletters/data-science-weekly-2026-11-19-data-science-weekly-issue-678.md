Richard McElreath illustrates Bayesian updating by means of “globe tossing”. Suppose you wish to know what proportion of the earth’s surface is covered in water, and you only have access to a globe and a blindfold. One way to learn the proportion is to pick random locations on the globe, and every time assess whether or not that location is covered in water. As the samples accumulate, your knowledge about the proportion of interest will be increasingly precise. Of course we need to start with a prior distribution; for a proportion, the most convenient choice is a beta distribution. Interim Analysis: My Messy Notes [ https://substack.com/redirect/1007bc44-2c5b-49fa-876f-b806e24cadd6?j=eyJ1IjoiMWxucmoifQ.TOO4JddZ2aOkng20GMCR3ePCcghfMgNuGHoEYV3w1tc ]
An interim analysis is a planned look at the data half way through a study, before all participants have been enrolled or followed up. They do it for practical and ethical reasons: if a treatment is clearly working, or clearly harmful, it isn’t right to keep randomizing people to the worse option, and if it’s clearly going nowhere, they can save time and resources by stopping early. The catch is that every extra look is another chance to find a “significant” result purely by luck. Peeking twice at the usual 5% threshold pushes the false-positive rate up. Interim analysis methods, such as O’Brien-Fleming boundaries, fix this by setting a stricter bar at the early looks so that the overall error rate stays at 5%…Now you know me, I’m the see-it-to-believe kind of person. Let’s try to simulate this with multiple trials and see if that’s true…
