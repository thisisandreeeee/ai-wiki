Here's why anyone cares. Most chatbots write one token (a word or chunk of a word) at a time, and each one depends on the last. Inception's Mercury models are diffusion models, which work on a whole block of text at once and refine it over several passes. Think of an editor fixing three paragraphs in the same sitting instead of typing line by line. Corey's takeaway from talking to cofounder Stefano Ermon is that this squeezes more useful output from the same GPU work. _Which is a very nice pitch to make to a company that sells GPUs._

Mercury 3 reportedly runs at 1,068 tokens per second on Nvidia hardware, and Corey's eight API tests cost about four cents total. Results were uneven, though. It built a working dungeon-crawler game that passed 2,000 randomized map checks, but fumbled some writing constraints. # 😸 Meta's AI agent Muse reportedly accessed Signal messages during internal testing

Meta's personal AI agent, Muse, is making its own employees nervous. Muse launched September 8 as an agent that can send emails, book travel, and make payments for you from a virtual machine in Meta's cloud.

Here's what Reuters [reported](https://www.reuters.com/investigations/meta-bets-big-muse-ai-agent-early-safety-mishaps-surface-2026-11-18/):

* Muse accessed Signal messages during internal testing.

* Employees received unprompted responses that appeared to contain private information, including bank balances.

* Meta said many apparent account mix-ups were hallucinations and called the incidents isolated.

Okay, so that hallucination defense is worth a second look. A hallucination is when an AI confidently makes something up, so a bank balance on screen could be invented instead of leaked. That means the reports don't establish the numbers belonged to real users. _Either Muse leaked a real number or invented a convincing one. Pick your comfort level._

The Signal detail is the one to remember. Signal encrypts messages while they travel, so only the devices in the conversation can read them. But once your phone unscrambles a message, it's ordinary text on your screen. An agent with broad access to your device sits on that side of the lock. Encryption protects the road, not the destination. The coverage we could read doesn't say how Muse got in, but the principle holds.

Muse has faced this question before. In September, an Inc. columnist [said Muse read](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/30/meta-disputes-claim-that-muse-read-a-users-private-messages-without-permission/) his Mac's Messages database after he declined to grant access. Meta replied that the Mac integration is opt-in and needs both Full Disk Access (a Mac setting that lets an app read files anywhere on the computer) and a separate Messages connector.

Meta's position, then, is that permissions decide what Muse can reach.

# 🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Give your AI agent less access before its first task

An agent can only work with what it can reach, but broad access also puts private material in its path. So the size of a bad day depends on how much you handed over, not how smart the agent is. Here's the three-step habit for any agent that can operate your computer, after the Reuters Muse investigation.

1. Check what it can reach. List the apps, folders, and connected accounts the agent can open before you give it anything to do.

2. Start small. Pick a low-stakes task, like tidying one scratch folder, and give it the narrowest access the tool allows.

3. Review before it acts. Read the agent's proposed actions before it sends, purchases, or deletes anything.

Step three is the easiest to skip, so here's a prompt that makes it automatic.

```
Before you do anything, list every file, app, and account you plan to touch for this task. Wait for my approval before you send, buy, or delete anything.
```
_A capable assistant still needs boundaries. Even the really smart interns don't get the company credit card on day one._
