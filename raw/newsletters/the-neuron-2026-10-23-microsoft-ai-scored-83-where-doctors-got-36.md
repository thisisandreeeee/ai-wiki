# 😺 Microsoft's AI Beat Doctors on Hard Medical Cases. The Fine Print Matters.

Picture a case that stumped the hospital: odd symptoms, no obvious cause. [Microsoft AI says](https://microsoft.ai/news/building-medical-superintelligence/) its new system, MAI-DxO, solved **83%** of a set of hard cases from the New England Journal of Medicine. The doctors in the same test got **36%**.

MAI-DxO (short for Microsoft AI Diagnostic Orchestrator) works like a panel of virtual doctors. Each one proposes diagnoses, picks which questions to ask or tests to order, and challenges the group's emerging answer, over several rounds. It also weighs what each test would cost, so it can't just order everything.

The test matters as much as the score. Medical exams hand you the whole case up front, and real doctors never get that. So Microsoft built SDBench (Sequential Diagnosis Benchmark), where the player starts with limited information and decides what to ask or order next, one step at a time. It covers 304 hard NEJM cases plus more typical emergency-room cases.

Here's how the scores shook out:

* Hard NEJM cases: MAI-DxO hit 83%, versus 36% for physicians with textbooks and internet access and 22% without.

* Typical ER cases: MAI-DxO hit 80%, versus 47% for physicians.

* Cost: Microsoft says it reached the right diagnosis at roughly half to a third of the cost in the NEJM work, with fewer tests.

Now the fine print, which is where our eyebrows went up. [TechCrunch's write-up](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/22/microsofts-mustafa-suleyman-says-its-ai-outperforms-doctors-on-hard-cases-as-he-chases-medical-superintelligence/) flags a few things worth knowing before you picture a robot doctor:

* The doctors were primary care physicians and general internists working alone, not the specialists and teams who usually handle cases this hard.

* It was a simulation with no real patients, and Microsoft says real-world performance is still to be tested.

* Most of the gain comes from the AI models underneath. GPT-5 alone scored 78.9%, and MAI-DxO added about four points on top.

_Microsoft's star diagnostic panel does its best work on OpenAI's models. Family dinner must be fun._

Still, the panel idea is the part worth stealing. Getting several AI agents to argue before they answer beat a single answer, and that trick will probably show up anywhere the stakes are high, like law and finance.

What comes next is the real test. Microsoft is teaming up with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston for a study in emergency and urgent care. Patients get standard care, consenting patients also talk to the system, and researchers compare its recommendations with the human doctors'. Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman is pitching all this as a step toward medical superintelligence, and Microsoft plans a healthcare model with Mayo Clinic next year. There's no pricing or availability for MAI-DxO itself yet.

The number to watch comes from Boston: how often the panel's call matches a real ER doctor's, with real patients in the room.

# 🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Forecast Your Real Launch Date With a Monte Carlo Simulation

Every project plan has a finish date, and most of them are a polite fiction. Plans add up each task's most likely duration, but real tasks run far later than they ever run early.

We tested a five-task launch: design then build, legal review then content, and QA at the end. The most likely estimates add up to 18 days. Simulated 10,000 times, it hit 18 days or less only about 10% of the time. The median finish was around 21 days, and 9 times out of 10 it was done by roughly 24 to 25.

That 18-day date is a plan, not a forecast. A Monte Carlo simulation (rolling the dice 10,000 times and counting) turns one date into odds, and ChatGPT can run it from a spreadsheet in minutes.

It also shows where to spend your worry. Build and design drove most of the swing, while legal review barely moved the date. _You can stop losing sleep over the lawyers. Probably._

Try it:

1. Build a simple sheet with one row per task and these columns: Task, Best case, Most likely, Worst case (in working days), and Depends on. Ask the people doing the work for all three numbers, because the worst case is where the truth hides. Our example: Design 3/5/13, Build 8/10/20, Legal review 4/6/15, Content 5/7/12, and QA and launch prep 2/3/6.

2. Upload the sheet to [ChatGPT's data analysis tool](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8437071-data-analysis-with-chatgpt) and paste the prompt below, filling in your current planned finish. Using Gemini instead? Make sure code execution is on and that it shows you the code it ran, because a description of a simulation is not a simulation.

3. Read the odds, not the average. Plan around the P80 (the date you hit 8 times out of 10), then aim your risk mitigation at the top two tasks in the sensitivity chart.

```
You are a project risk analyst. I've uploaded a project schedule with columns: Task, Best case, Most likely, Worst case (all in working days), and Depends on.

Run a Monte Carlo schedule simulation:
- Model each task's duration with a PERT-beta distribution built from its three estimates.
- Respect the Depends on column and calculate the project finish for every run. The critical path can change from run to run.
- Run 10,000 simulations.

Then give me:
- The P50, P80, and P90 finish in working days, plus the chance of finishing by my current planned finish of [X] days.
- A bar chart ranking which tasks' durations drive the finish date the most.
- The percentage of runs where each path was the critical one.
- A 30-second plain-English summary I can paste into a stakeholder update.

Before running anything, list every assumption you're making (distribution, task independence, working days, no resource limits). Show me the Python code you used so I can check it. If any row looks wrong, for example a most likely value outside the best-to-worst range, flag it instead of silently fixing it.
```
Two cautions. The default setup treats tasks as independent, so if the same overloaded team owns three of them, the real risk is higher than the simulation says. And the odds are only as good as the estimates, so skim the code and assumptions before you quote any number to your boss.
