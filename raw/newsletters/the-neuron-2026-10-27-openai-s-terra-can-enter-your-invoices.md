# 😸 OpenAI Says Its AI Disproved a Famous Computer Science Hypothesis, Then Launched GPT 6.1 Terra

On Monday, OpenAI put out two announcements that look like they came from different companies. One reports [three new discoveries](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/) in math, computer science, and physics. The other introduces [GPT-6.1 Terra](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-1-terra/), a cheaper model built to click through office software.

Here's what happened:

* OpenAI says its models proved the local smoothing conjecture, a 1991 math problem about how waves focus, in every dimension.

* It says its system disproved the Exponential Time Hypothesis, and a proof-checking program called Lean verified the result.

* It says its models produced a rigorous existence proof for quantum chromodynamics, the theory of the strong force.

* Terra costs $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens, half the price of GPT-6.1 Sol.

Start with the computer science result, because it comes with the weirdest cast of characters. The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) is a belief about a puzzle called Boolean satisfiability: given a pile of true/false switches and a list of rules, can you find a setting that satisfies every rule? The best known methods get exponentially slower as you add switches, and the ETH says nothing fundamentally faster exists.

Hundreds of papers use it as a starting assumption, in fields from databases to computational biology. If the ETH is false, a lot of "can't be done faster" results become open questions again.

OpenAI says its system found the disproof on October 16, and Lean checked it the next day. Lean works like a spellchecker for logic: it confirms every step follows from the last. A working group of experts now has to confirm the claim means what we think it means. It's led by Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi, and Ryan Williams, and Impagliazzo and Paturi are two of the three people who originally proposed the ETH. _So the authors of the hypothesis are now deciding whether a robot just knocked it down. That's a fun group chat._

OpenAI is upfront about how raw this is. It calls the manuscripts raw research data, and it says turning a disproved ETH into faster software will take significant further research. A group led by Nobel laureate David Gross is also holding back the physics result while it writes its own account.

**Same day, very different launch: GPT-6.1 Terra.** Terra is the middle model in OpenAI's lineup, built for computer use, meaning it looks at a screen, then clicks, types, and moves between apps like a person would. OpenAI's own example is reading incoming invoices and entering the details into accounting software. _Riveting? No. Valuable? Ask anyone who has keyed invoice numbers by hand on a Friday afternoon._

OpenAI claims Terra scores 15 points higher than its predecessor on its Agents' Last Exam at about a third of the cost. It says Terra matches Astra at one-tenth the price per token and finishes over three times faster than Astra and Sol. An independent [NerdsChalk test](https://nerdschalk.com/gpt-6-1-terra-chatgpt-codex-availability/) saw a smaller gap, with Sol taking about 1.8 times as long on three short tasks. [An OpenAI engineer](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/26/openai-rolls-out-budget-priced-mid-tier-gpt-61-terra/5305574) called it more of a way to stretch usage limits than a bombshell.

Both launches share one problem: how do you check the work? A filled-out form takes seconds to verify. A proof of a 35-year-old conjecture takes months, a working group, and a conference OpenAI has agreed to fund. Watch for that group's verdict on the ETH, since it's the cleanest test yet of whether AI-generated proofs hold up when the people who built the field take a look.

# 🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Race Two Models on the Same Boring Task

OpenAI's Victor Nunez [raced Terra against Sol](https://x.com/victornunez/status/2114781066892378396) to show off Terra's computer use. He had each model fill out the same long web form in Fast mode while the screen recorded, and according to [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20261027-gpt-6-1-terra/), Terra finished more than two minutes sooner.

NerdsChalk ran a smaller version in Codex: a 15-field form, three runs per model. Terra and Sol both got every field right every time, and Terra's median time was 9.4 seconds to Sol's 17.3. The cheapest model, Luna, got every field right in only one of three runs.

That result is the lesson. OpenAI says Terra is over three times faster on its benchmark, but NerdsChalk's gap was closer to 1.8 times, and the model with the lowest price slipped on accuracy. Vendor numbers are averages across their tasks, and your task is the one that matters. You can run the same race on any chore you'd hand to AI:

1. Pick a task you can grade, like filling a form with fake data, cleaning ten spreadsheet rows, or logging five sample invoices. Use a throwaway form and dummy data, since agents that click around the web can be tricked by what's on a page.

2. Run identical instructions three times on a cheaper model and three times on a pricier one. In ChatGPT Work or Codex, Terra shows up in the model picker for Plus, Pro, Enterprise, and Edu plans.

3. Score two things: how many runs came out fully right, and how long each took. Switch to the cheaper model only if accuracy holds.

```
Fill out this form using the fake data below. Do not submit it. When you finish, list any field you were unsure about and tell me how many steps it took.
```
_Think of it as a taste test, except this one has a stopwatch._ Three runs won't settle anything scientific, but a 9-second versus 17-second gap on your own chore tells you what the benchmark chart can't, and one wrong field in three runs tells you the cheap model isn't ready for that job yet.

Anthropic just filed to go public, and the paperwork doubles as a forecast: within a few years, AI could match top human experts.

Here’s what they’re predicting:

* AI that matches or beats the best experts in most professional and scientific fields.

* AI that works alone for hours, days, or weeks, then checks in for feedback.

* AI that runs lab equipment and robots through digital controls.

Anthropic’s name for the endgame is “a country of geniuses in a data center,” meaning millions of AI agents working at once on software, medicine, physics, and more.

Why take it seriously? Anthropic says it’s already living it. Over 90% of September’s code contributions were written by Claude, and each engineer ships over 45x the daily code they did in 2024. Revenue is up about 1,200% in a year while headcount rose about 170%.

_It’s a flywheel: better models help build the next, better model. Anthropic calls it “closing the loop.”_

It also expects demand to outrun computing power for a while, since the economy has barely tried what today’s models can do.

Big caveat: this is a company’s own forecast, in a document meant to attract investors. _Read it like a pitch deck, not a weather report._
