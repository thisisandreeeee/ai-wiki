# 😺 Voters push back on data centers, and AI companies offer them a deal

Imagine a company wants to build a giant facility full of computers near your house. It promises jobs and investment. You just want to know whether your electric bill is going up.

That’s the conversation AI companies are having with towns across America. And this week, voters gave them a pretty clear answer: you haven’t convinced us yet.

In Alhambra, California, 83% voted to ban data centers. In Wells, Maine, 82% backed a 180-day pause. [Ballotpedia counted](https://ballotpedia.org/Data_center-related_ballot_measures,_2026) 46 of 47 local data-center votes this year going toward tighter limits. That includes votes before Tuesday, with communities approving restrictions or rejecting proposals that would help development.

Two days after the midterms, OpenAI [announced new commitments](https://openai.com/index/setting-new-standard-responsible-ai-infrastructure/) to the towns hosting its future data centers. Residents would get more say in the plans. The company would cover its power costs and invest in things the community actually wants.

Okay, but shouldn’t it already be paying its electric bill?

The argument is over everything needed to deliver that electricity. A data center can require new power lines and other grid upgrades. Depending on how those costs get divided, nearby households could help pay for them through their utility bills.

An October [Senate investigation](https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/ai-data-center-companies-reveal-to-warren-blumenthal-van-hollen-they-are-not-paying-their-full-costs-will-continue-using-ndas-and-seeking-tax-breaks/) found companies disputed how much they should pay for shared-grid upgrades triggered by their projects. _You can cancel ChatGPT Plus. Getting its infrastructure off your electric bill is a little harder._

Here’s what OpenAI promised:

* Pay for electricity and infrastructure, with financial guarantees so residents aren’t left covering costs if a project falls through.

* Publish each site’s water and power use, noise studies, and local hiring information.

* Stop requiring confidentiality agreements from public officials, except for narrow security and proprietary information protections.

* Give residents input while plans can still change, and fund priorities they help choose.

There’s money attached, too. OpenAI [pledged local investments](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/05/technology/openai-data-centers-midterms.html) of $80 million in Effingham County, Georgia, and $40 million in Pike County, Ohio.

Other companies have been making their own offers. [Microsoft pledged in January](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2026/01/13/community-first-ai-infrastructure/) to cover the electricity infrastructure needed for its data centers and stop asking towns for lower property tax rates. Amazon’s [Built Together initiative](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-data-centers-built-together) promises more than $1 billion over five years for education, job training, energy efficiency, and other local priorities.

**Why this matters**: The catch is which buildings count. OpenAI’s promises cover future sites it owns and operates. Partners such as Oracle own much of the computing capacity they use, so those facilities don’t automatically receive the same commitments.

The part I’d watch is what gets put in writing and who can enforce it. If construction stops, who pays? If the promised benefits don’t arrive, what can the town do?

Voters have shown they can say no. Now AI companies have to give them a reason to say yes.

# 🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Turn survey responses into your next three moves

You collected the feedback. Now comes the part everyone puts off: figuring out what to do with it.

Google’s new [Actionable Insights in Forms](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/get-actionable-insights-with-gemini-in-Google-Forms.html) uses Gemini to find patterns across survey questions and recommend next steps. Think spotting what disappointed first-time attendees at your event, instead of just reading the average satisfaction score.

It’s rolling out to eligible paid plans. Your form needs at least 12 responses and three questions, according to [Google’s instructions](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/16231981).

Here’s how to try it:

1. Open a Google Form and click Responses.

2. Find Actionable Insights, then click Generate insights.

3. Expand Analysis steps to review how it reached its conclusions.

Want a more focused plan? Copy the findings into Gemini and try this prompt:

```
Based on these survey findings, recommend three actions we can take next week. For each, identify the supporting evidence, likely effort, and one assumption we should check. Separate recurring complaints from isolated comments.

Survey findings: [paste here]
```
Check the recommendations against the original responses before acting. The useful skill is connecting feedback to a decision you can actually make.
