# 😸 Anthropic Released Claude Fable 5.2 and a Restricted Version Called Mythos 5.2

Anthropic [released Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2) Monday. They're the same underlying AI model, but come with different safety rules. Anthropic says both generate output up to 30% faster than their 5.1 predecessors. Fable costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, 20% below Fable 5.1.

Fable is available broadly, but it blocks many high-risk cybersecurity and research biology requests. Mythos is for vetted security and life-sciences organizations that need those capabilities. When Fable refuses a request, Anthropic says an older model handles it instead. Fable reportedly blocks 55% fewer requests than Fable 5.1. _Same engine, different keys._

View image: (https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80/uploads/asset/file/818c24af-dca5-43e2-a66a-aa39bd68d672/frontiercode-2-0-accuracy-vs-cost.png?t=1794286835)
Follow image link: (https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2#:~:text=Agentic%20coding%3A%20FrontierCode,engineering%3A%20BenchCAD%202.1)
Caption: Anthropic’s own FrontierCode 2.0 chart plots each model’s score (how often its code changes would be accepted by open-source maintainers) against the cost of each task. At High effort, Fable 5.2 lands about even with Opus 5.5’s best score at roughly half the cost. At Xhigh effort it reaches 49.1%, the highest score Anthropic has recorded for a released model. Max effort costs over three times as much as Xhigh and scores lower.

The capabilities sound wild, and Anthropic supplied the examples. On its own evaluations, Fable beat Opus 5.5 on nearly every tested capability, though Anthropic still recommends Opus 5.5 as the starting point for most coding and knowledge work.

Here are two demos worth unpacking:

* Engineers used more than 1,000 subagents, copies of the model splitting a big task into smaller jobs, to build a prototype operating system from scratch. After eight days, it booted on a laptop.

* Fable formalized the existing proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean, a language that checks each mathematical step. That's translating a known proof into machine-checkable logic, not discovering a new theorem. Anthropic says it took 30 hours; independent confirmation remains unclear.

Here's the harder question: can Anthropic reliably test a system this capable? In its safety trials, Mythos 5.2 broke sandbox rules 3.1% of the time, versus 21% for Mythos 5.1. That's progress, but in up to 43% of audit transcripts, the model appeared to recognize it was being tested. It also slipped past a monitor when instructed to underperform roughly one time in four.

**Our take:** The demos are impressive, but they're largely Anthropic's claims, and its named outside testers didn't review the launch blog or system card. _A model that knows when the teacher is watching makes the test a lot harder to grade._ What matters next is independent verification of both the capability claims and the safety checks.

# 🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Test whether your local AI app is actually offline

Say you set up an AI app to run on a local model so nothing leaves your machine. Then you move it to a computer with no internet, and it works fine during setup. On the first real task, it hangs.

Here's why. Many open-source AI libraries download helper files the first time you use them, like tokenizers (the lookup tables that chop text into pieces a model can read), embedding models, or browser components. Some also send usage data back to their makers, which is called telemetry. None of this shows up in a normal install, so an app can look local and still need the internet.

Humanet [tested 20 open-source AI repositories](https://github.com/humanethq/airgap-check) for exactly this, tuning its checker on 10 and then running it on 10 it hadn't seen. Nine of those ten had first-use downloads, and the checker flagged all nine. It also raised 4 false alarms and missed 5 individual downloaders (including some OCR libraries). _So a clean result only vouches for the paths you tested, and the numbers are self-reported by the project._

The tool is airgap-check, an open-source command line program that needs Docker for step two. It works in three passes.

1. Scan the code for likely downloaders, telemetry packages, and the names of secret keys in your .env file (it reads the names, not the values).

```
   airgap-check /path/to/repo
```
2. Run your smoke test (a quick check that the main workflows work) inside a container, which is a sealed sandbox, with networking switched off. It records every outbound connection the app tries. Point the test at the workflows you plan to use, since it only catches what your test touches.

```
   airgap-check --run --image my-app:latest --run-cmd "python3 -m pytest tests/smoke_test.py" /path/to/repo
```
3. Fix what it finds. The fix flag writes a Dockerfile block that downloads the needed models and assets while you build the image, so nothing gets fetched at runtime. Then repeat step 2.

```
   airgap-check --fix /path/to/repo
```
If you're not the one at the terminal, hand this to whoever deploys your AI tools, and run it before the app lands on a hospital network or a machine holding sensitive files. The repo has the install steps and the full benchmark breakdown.

# 🔧 Tuesday Tool Tip: Use Claude to follow the money in your community

Ever looked at your local government's budget and wondered where all that money actually goes? Anthropic's new [Claude Fable 5.2](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2) might help you find answers.

In early testing, Fable caught financial figures that didn't add up and handled complex financial analysis. Here's how to put those capabilities to work for something that affects your community:

1. Find your city or town's proposed budget and last year's spending report.

2. Open Claude, select Fable 5.2 if available, and upload both documents.

3. Give it this prompt:

“Compare how our local government is allocating money this year versus last year. Identify the biggest spending increases and cuts, recalculate totals where possible, and explain which public services are affected. Cite the exact page for every finding, flag missing information, and suggest five questions residents should ask at the next public budget hearing. Don't invent figures.“ — 

You'll get a starting point for understanding public spending, spotting discrepancies, and asking better questions about decisions that affect your neighborhood.

Just verify the numbers against the original documents before sharing them. Even a very smart AI shouldn't get the final say on where your taxes went.
